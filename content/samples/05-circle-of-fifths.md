The circle of fifths arranges all twelve major keys so that each step clockwise moves up a **perfect fifth** — and adds exactly one sharp to the key signature. Once you understand it, key signatures stop being something to memorise.

<!-- more -->

## Going clockwise: sharps

Start at **C major** (no sharps or flats) at the top. Each clockwise step goes up a fifth:

| Key | Sharps |
|---|---|
| C | 0 |
| G | 1 — F♯ |
| D | 2 — F♯ C♯ |
| A | 3 — F♯ C♯ G♯ |
| E | 4 |
| B | 5 |
| F♯ | 6 |
| C♯ | 7 |

Sharps are always added in the same order: **F C G D A E B** — "Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle".

## Going anticlockwise: flats

Moving the other way goes *down* a fifth and adds a flat each time: **F** (1♭), **B♭** (2), **E♭** (3), **A♭** (4), **D♭** (5), **G♭** (6), **C♭** (7).

Flats appear in the reverse order: **B E A D G C F** — "Battle Ends And Down Goes Charles' Father".

## Relative minors

Every major key shares its key signature with a **relative minor**, found three semitones below it: C major ↔ A minor, G major ↔ E minor, F major ↔ D minor. On most diagrams these sit on an inner ring.

## Why neighbouring keys sound good together

Keys next to each other on the circle share six of their seven notes. That is why the chords built on neighbouring keys — I, IV and V — form the backbone of countless songs. Here is the famous **I–V–vi–IV** progression in C major:

```abc
X:1
T:I–V–vi–IV in C major
M:4/4
L:1/2
K:C
"C"[CEG]2 | "G"[B,DG]2 | "Am"[A,CE]2 | "F"[A,CF]2 |]
```

> [!TIP]
> To find the I, IV and V chords of any key, look at the key on the circle and its two neighbours: the one anticlockwise is IV, the one clockwise is V.

## Practise it

1. How many sharps does **A major** have? *(3)*
2. What is the relative minor of **E♭ major**? *(C minor)*
3. Which chords are I, IV and V in **G major**? *(G, C, D)*
